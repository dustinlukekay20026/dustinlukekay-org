Organizing relief after a Denison house fire

Community Relief Organizer · January 2023

In January 2023, I helped organize immediate support for a North Texas family displaced by a house fire. That meant helping arrange a temporary hotel stay, providing food, and bringing in other people who could help. The first priority was getting the family a place to stay and something to eat while they dealt with what came next.

From classmates to family: Denison community rallies around mom, veteran caregiver after losing home to fire

KXII-TV · Lauren Rangel · January 25, 2023

KXII reported how neighbors, friends, and former classmates rallied around Jana Jones, a veteran caregiver and mother whose family lost their home in a fire. Neighbors rescued her son, and I reached out after seeing her post. I helped coordinate temporary lodging, food, and other essentials and launched a GoFundMe that raised more than $2,000, according to KXII.

Helping respond to the 1997 Truckee River flood

U.S. Navy humanitarian relief · Naval Air Station Fallon, Nevada

In January 1997, I was stationed at NAS Fallon when heavy rain and melting Sierra Nevada snow drove flooding along the Truckee River. The flood affected communities in western Nevada, including the Reno–Sparks area.

I took part in the relief response while serving in the Navy and received the Humanitarian Service Medal for my participation. The Truckee River flood was an early chapter in my disaster-relief work, years before I volunteered in North Texas after tornadoes and helped organize support for a family displaced by a house fire.

Why helping our neighbors matters

When a former classmate lost her home in a fire, she had to figure out where her family would sleep and how they would get through the next few days. I could help answer some of those questions. I helped arrange temporary lodging and food, reached out to former classmates and neighbors, and started a fundraiser. I couldn’t undo what happened, but I could take a few decisions off her plate at a time when every decision was harder than it should have been.

I saw a different side of that work during the Truckee River flood while stationed at NAS Fallon and again while clearing tornado debris in North Texas. Recovery depends on people doing jobs that are easy to overlook. Someone has to sort clothing, get supplies where they’re needed, or spend an afternoon clearing debris. Those tasks matter because they let a family put its time and energy toward the decisions only that family can make.

When my family and I prepare meals and care packages, the most important part is listening. A person may need a meal that day, clothing that fits, or supplies for the dog they care for. Asking what would help gives them a say in what happens next. It also teaches us to pay attention to needs we might otherwise miss.

One reason I keep volunteering is that showing up gives other people a way to join in. A former classmate can make a call. A neighbor can help with a meal. A family can spend an afternoon gathering supplies. When someone asks me where to start, I tell them to ask what would help, offer something they can actually do, and follow through. The follow-through is what makes the offer count.
